Hydroponics is a method of growing plants without soil, using water enriched with dissolved nutrients instead. Plant roots grow directly in the nutrient solution or in an inert growing medium (such as rockwool, perlite, or clay pebbles) that the solution flows through. The six major hydroponic systems are deep water culture (roots submerged in aerated solution), nutrient film technique (thin film of solution flows over roots), ebb and flow (periodic flooding), drip systems, wick systems, and aeroponics (roots misted with nutrient spray). Hydroponics can produce plants 20–50% faster than soil growing and uses 90% less water because the system recirculates the nutrient solution. Because plants get nutrients directly in dissolved form, they spend less energy growing roots and more energy on above-ground growth. Hydroponics requires precise management of pH (typically 5.5–6.5) and nutrient concentration (electrical conductivity). Common crops grown hydroponically include lettuce, tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, herbs, and cannabis. Aeroponics is a subset of soilless cultivation but differs from hydroponics in that roots hang in air rather than water. Compared to aeroponics, hydroponics is generally more forgiving mechanically — a pump failure is slower to kill plants since roots are in liquid. Vertical hydroponic farms in urban areas can produce food year-round with minimal land use.
